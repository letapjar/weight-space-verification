# What this is: a small trained neural network, most likely the policy of a paddle game on a grid

**Short answer:** This is a trained 3-layer MLP (500 → 8 → 512 → 5) that maps a 10×10 grid observation to 5 action scores. The weights fit a Pong/squash-style game. An agent confined to the left column moves **up or down** to line up with a ball, and the network has learned roughly **"is the ball above or below my paddle?"** Of the five outputs, only two are really distinct. The other three behave as identical "don't move" actions.

The data has no labels, so everything below is inferred from the weights alone. I separate the solid conclusions from the plausible ones.

---

## 1. Architecture

| Layer | Shape | Params |
|---|---|---|
| W1, b1 | 500 → 8 | 4,008 |
| W2, b2 | 8 → 512 | 4,608 |
| W3, b3 | 512 → 5 | 2,565 |
| **Total** | | **11,181** |

- The layer order is unusual: an 8-unit **bottleneck** followed by a wide 512-unit layer. All information passes through 8 numbers, so the 512 units are heavily redundant.
- The untrained-looking weights have std ≈ 0.06. That matches Glorot/Xavier init for these fan-ins (√(2/508) ≈ √(2/520) ≈ √(2/517) ≈ 0.062). Biases are all small (|b| ≲ 0.16), consistent with zero init plus modest training.
- **Most of W1 is still at init.** Only about 130 of the 500 input rows have clearly trained weights (up to |w| ≈ 1.7). Which rows are trained is the main clue to what the input is.

## 2. The input is a 10×10 grid with 5 features per cell (solid inference)

The trained rows of W1 fall in a regular pattern:

- They repeat with **period 50**: indices ~55–94, ~105–144, … ~405–444.
- Inside each block they alternate gaps of 2, 3, 2, 3 (e.g. 61, 64, 66, 69, 71, 74…). That is **period 5, with offsets 1 and 4 active**.

This matches a flattened grid: `index = (row·10 + col)·5 + channel`, with 10 rows, 10 columns and 5 channels.

Mapped back onto the grid:

- **Row 0, row 9, column 0 and column 9 are all untrained.** These are border cells whose input never varies, i.e. **walls**. The playfield is the **8×8 interior**.
- **Channel 0** is trained **only in column 1**, rows 1–8. Something lives only in the leftmost playable column. This is the **agent/paddle**.
- **Channel 1** is trained in columns **2–8**, never column 1. This is a moving object that appears everywhere except the paddle column, i.e. the **ball**. It would never show in column 1 if it is drawn as "paddle" when it hits and the episode ends when it misses.
- **Channel 4** is trained in columns 1–8. It is a second sparse, changing feature. Its weights don't share a common component across cells, so it is probably not an "empty cell" indicator. The likeliest readings are the **ball's previous position or trail**, which would give velocity information, or a second object. This is the least certain part.
- **Channels 2 and 3** are untrained everywhere. Those features are either constant (e.g. "wall" at the border) or never occur.

## 3. What the network learned from the grid

Most trained rows of W1 point along one shared direction in the 8-d hidden space (ignoring h1 and h7):

- **Pattern A**: h0 and h3 positive; h2, h4, h5, h6 negative.
- **Pattern B**: the opposite.

Example rows:
- (row 8, col 5) ch4: `1.71 0.47 −1.42 1.72 −1.63 −1.57 −1.61 −0.54` is pattern A.
- (row 6, col 3) ch1: `−1.16 1.40 1.20 −1.22 0.99 1.11 0.86 −0.71` is pattern B.

**Ball (channel 1):**
- In columns 2–4, nearest the paddle, the split is clean: **rows 1–4 give A and rows 5–8 give B**. The top half of the field pushes one way and the bottom half the other.
- Toward the far right (columns 7–8) the weights shrink to near zero and the pattern gets messier. That fits a bouncing ball: when it is far away, its current row says little about where it will arrive.

**Paddle (channel 0, column 1) has the reverse sign:**
- Paddle in rows 3–4 gives B (e.g. index 155: `−0.38 0.33 0.37 −0.36 …`).
- Paddle in rows 6–7 gives A (e.g. index 305: `0.17 −0.29 −0.31 0.33 …`).

So the A–B axis encodes roughly **ball height minus paddle height**, the relative vertical offset. That is exactly what a paddle controller needs.

**Other hidden units:**
- **h1** feeds the units that *promote* both up and down moves, and inhibits the units that *suppress* them. It acts like a "move vs. stay" gain signal.
- **h7** has large weights in W1, but its outgoing row in W2 is still init-sized (|w| ≲ 0.11). Layer 2 effectively ignores it, so it is a wasted, probably dead, unit.

## 4. Layer 2: 512 redundant detectors

The trained units fall into four groups, judged by their W3 rows:

| Type | Example units (W3 row) | Input from bottleneck |
|---|---|---|
| Promote **output 1** | 9 (`+1.10`), 36 (`+0.93`), 51 (`+0.65`) | Active on A (e.g. unit 9 has −0.51 from h5) |
| Suppress **output 1** | 57 (`−1.17`) | Active on B (+0.58 from h5) |
| Promote **output 2** | 37 (`+0.66`), 26 (`+0.45`) | Active on B |
| Suppress **output 2** | 3 (`−0.75`), 23 (`−0.52`), 41 (`−0.52`) | Active on A |

- No unit drives both outputs 1 and 2. Each unit is "pure".
- Many units still have init-sized output weights.
- The 512-wide layer mainly re-encodes a 2–3-dimensional signal many times over.

## 5. Outputs: effectively 3 actions, not 5

- **Columns 0, 3 and 4 of W3 are almost identical in every row.** For example, row 3 is `0.131 / 0.127 / 0.123` and row 57 is `0.126 / 0.122 / 0.133`. The network cannot tell these three actions apart.
- **Columns 1 and 2** carry all the decision-specific structure.
- Biases b3 are `0.161, −0.276, −0.285, 0.142, 0.057`. With no strong signal, the three equivalent actions are preferred, i.e. **stay put**.

This fits the common 5-action gridworld order **[NOOP, UP, DOWN, LEFT, RIGHT]**:

- A paddle locked to column 1 can only move vertically, so LEFT and RIGHT do the same thing as NOOP. The training signal treats all three identically, and their weights converge.
- The directions agree with this reading. Pattern A (ball in upper rows, or paddle low) drives output 1 (UP). Pattern B (ball low, or paddle high) drives output 2 (DOWN). This assumes row 1 is the top of the screen.

## 6. Policy or Q-network?

I lean toward **softmax policy logits**, e.g. from REINFORCE, A2C or PPO:

- In a DQN, all five Q-values share a large state-value component, so you'd expect strong W3 rows with the same sign across all five columns. There are essentially none here. Under a softmax, that shared component is irrelevant and never gets trained.
- Supervised imitation of a scripted expert is less likely. In that case NOOP (sometimes a label) would diverge from LEFT/RIGHT (never labels). Here the three stay identical.

A Q-network is still possible, especially with rewards near zero, so treat this as a lean, not a certainty.

## 7. Overall picture and caveats

**Overall:** this is a small trained agent for a single-player Pong/squash-like game on a 10×10 walled grid. The paddle moves vertically in the left column and the ball bounces around the 8×8 field. The network:

1. reads where the ball is (mostly its row, mostly when it's near) and where the paddle is,
2. compresses that into an "above vs. below" signal plus a "move vs. stay" signal in the 8-unit bottleneck (one of which, h7, is wasted),
3. expands it into hundreds of redundant UP/DOWN promote/suppress detectors,
4. outputs scores where UP and DOWN are real choices and the other three are interchangeable ways of staying still.

**Caveats:**
- The activation function isn't given. ReLU fits the one-sided unit specialization but isn't proven.
- Calling outputs 1 and 2 "up" and "down" depends on the action ordering and on row 1 being the top.
- Channel 4's meaning (previous ball position vs. something else) is genuinely uncertain.
- The paddle height isn't determinable. Paddle rows 1, 2, 5 and 8 have weak weights, which may mean it spans several cells.

Forward passes on hand-built grids (ball top-left with paddle low, and so on) would confirm or refute this quickly. I didn't run any, as you asked.
