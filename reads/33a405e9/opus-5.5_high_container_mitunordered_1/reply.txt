**Observations:**

**Layout of the inputs**
- The 500 rows fall into blocks of 5 consecutive inputs. Strong rows sit only at positions 0, 1 and 4 within a block (index mod 5). Rows that copy the bias sit only at positions 2 and 3.
- So the layout is cell-major: each block is one cell, and the channels are in the stated order. The cells themselves are not in raster order.

**Always-on inputs (rows that copy b1)**
- An input that is 1 in every state gets the same gradient as the bias, so its weight row ends up looking like b1. For example:
  - Row 8: `0.017 -0.402 -0.122 0.124 0.228 -0.301 …`
  - Row 23: `-0.055 -0.487 0.074 0.110 0.111 -0.146 …`
  - b1: `-0.006 -0.359 -0.019 0.076 0.161 -0.227 …`
- Exactly **36** such rows are at channel 3 (obstacle), for example rows 8, 23, 28, 38 and 53. That is the wall border: 100 − 64 = 36 cells.
- Exactly **8** more such rows are at channel 2 (reward): rows 12, 77, 107, 192, 267, 342, 362 and 367.
- In those 8 cells every other channel has tiny weights (for example rows 10, 11, 13 and 14 are all below 0.12). So 8 cells hold a reward target in every state, and the agent and moving objects never appear there. That looks like a fixed goal strip of 8 cells, the width of one inner row.

**Active cells**
- The remaining 56 cells carry the large weights.
- Exactly **8** of them have strong agent and previous-frame rows but a dead moving-object row. These are blocks 3, 13, 14, 28, 54, 60, 74 and 97, for example rows 15/16/19: `0.065 0.147 0.348 …` / `-0.005 -0.055 0.001 …` / `-0.026 0.373 -0.823 …`. That fits a safe row of 8 cells with no traffic.
- The other 48 cells (6 rows' worth) all have large moving-object weights. The largest single weights include row 100 (`-0.904 7.767 …`), row 445 (`-1.923 -2.696 -10.058 …`) and row 65 (`-2.618 -2.674 -7.836 …`).

**Duplicate rows**
- Many moving-object and previous-frame rows are near-copies of each other in different cells:
  - row 1 ≈ row 166 (`0.683 0.637 -0.015 0.872` vs `0.632 0.487 -0.038 0.806`)
  - row 124 ≈ row 174 (`… 4.028 2.514 …` vs `… 5.048 3.139 …`)
  - row 239 ≈ row 251 ≈ row 406
  - row 444 ≈ row 469
- Near-identical rows mean those inputs are always equal to each other. That happens when objects move deterministically and periodically, such as evenly spaced cars in a lane.

**Output layer**
- b3 = `[0.901, 0.253, -1.282, -0.415, 0.483]`. "Down" is strongly suppressed and no-op is the default.
- The W3 "up" column has the largest positive weights (3.689, 2.127, 1.756, 1.635, 1.586). It also has a strong negative weight (−3.427) from a unit that also drives left/right/no-op.
- So the policy moves upward, and waits or sidesteps when needed.

**Reasoning:**
- The board is: a fixed goal strip (8 cells), 6 strips of deterministic moving objects (48 cells), and a traffic-free strip (8 cells). Add an agent that mostly moves up and often waits. That is a lane-crossing game.
- Snake or Pac-Man-style collection is ruled out: the reward is fixed and permanent rather than respawning at random places, and Snake would need a multi-cell agent body.
- Pure dodging games (falling objects) are ruled out: they have no permanent goal row and no traffic-free strip.
- Breakout/Pong-style games are ruled out: the agent does not live on a single row, and down is avoided rather than used.
- Maze or gridworld navigation is ruled out: there are no internal obstacles (exactly 36 always-on obstacle inputs) and many moving objects.
- The strong no-op bias fits timing a crossing between vehicles.

**Game:** A Frogger / Freeway-style road-crossing game. The agent starts on a safe bottom row and crosses 6 lanes of horizontally moving vehicles to reach a goal row at the top.

**Description:** The agent moves up through lanes of deterministically moving cars and waits or sidesteps to avoid being hit. It gets reward for reaching the 8-cell goal row on the far side, after which it is reset.
