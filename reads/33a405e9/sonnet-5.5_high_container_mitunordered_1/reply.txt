**Observations:**
- Many W1 rows are near-zero noise (|w| about 0.1). These are inputs that are almost never informative, or are constant.
- About 36 rows share one nearly identical pattern, for example `-0.402 … 0.469 … -0.523 … -0.368`, with first entries around -0.35 to -0.5 and about +0.5 at unit 12. The count matches the 36 border cells in the always-on wall/obstacle channel. These rows act like a shared constant bias, and the network learned nothing position-specific about the walls.
- A set of rows has very large, individually distinct weights. Examples are `11.266 … 7.584`, `5.170 -1.557 6.511 … 8.300 8.386`, `-1.923 -2.696 -10.058 … 5.609`, `-1.624 -4.010 … -9.429 … -9.601`, `1.047 -1.501 7.138 …`, and `2.436 2.693 … 7.894 … 8.042`. Each cell has its own signature, so I read these as the agent-position channel. The policy depends strongly on exactly where the agent is.
- Several groups of 2–6 rows share almost the same vector, each with its own distinctive large entries:
  - `… 3.352 …`, `4.010`, `2.157`, `2.726`, `3.513`
  - `… -3.39 … 3.64 …` and `-3.26 … 3.77`
  - `-4.60`, `-4.67`, `-3.47`, `-3.53`, `-2.46`, `-2.67` at one unit
  - `5.30` and `5.34` at one unit
  - `-4.2` at unit 1 in 4 rows, with `5.99` or `5.46` at unit 3 in 2 of them
  - `-4.45` and `-4.43`
  
  These look like groups of cells that behave alike, such as separate lanes or rows of objects in the moving-object, target or previous-frame channels. A feature is shared across several positions and is not tied to one cell.
- I could not reliably recover the cell/channel order, and it may be shuffled, so I did not try to map individual rows to coordinates.
- Output layer, from W3 and b3:
  - Biases are no-op +0.90, up +0.25, down -1.28, left -0.42, right +0.48.
  - No-op is the default action and down is strongly suppressed.
  - Several hidden units drive up strongly (W3 up column: 3.69, 2.13, 1.76, 1.64, and others).
  - Several drive down or right and push down or left in opposition (for example the `-3.427` up weight with `2.433` left).
  - Standing still is a legitimate and frequently chosen action, while moving down is rarely right.

**Reasoning:**
- A moving-objects channel plus a previous-frame channel means velocity matters. The target is a static reward, and the obstacles and moving objects are hazards.
- Because the agent-position weights are so large and cell-specific, the policy depends on exactly where the agent is relative to hazards. A pure "run to the nearest pellet" game would give smoother, more translation-invariant weights.
- A high no-op bias suggests that waiting for a gap is often optimal. That fits timing-based dodging, not continuous pursuit.
- The up preference and down suppression suggest progress is directional, toward row 0.
- Snake is less likely because the downward-suppressed policy would not fit a free-roaming head. Breakout and Pong-style games are unlikely because the agent moves in all four directions.
- Pac-Man-style chase fits less well, since I would expect more symmetric directional preferences.

**Game:** A Frogger- or Freeway-style road-crossing game. The agent crosses lanes of moving traffic to reach a goal at the top, with walls on the sides and collisions ending the episode. My second guess is a "collect rewards while dodging moving enemies" grid game, similar to a simplified Pac-Man.

**Description:** The agent moves in four directions or waits, timing its moves to avoid moving hazards while heading up toward the reward target. Reward comes from reaching or collecting the target, and touching a moving object or obstacle ends the episode.

**Confidence:** 25%
