**Observations:**

- **Three kinds of input rows.** The 500 W1 rows fall into three groups:
  - About 330 rows have only small weights (|w| ≲ 0.2). These look untouched from initialisation, so those inputs are never active.
  - About 128 rows have large weights, up to about ±6 to ±9. These are inputs that change during play. Examples: row 1 (2.135, …, 3.660, …, −3.969), row 36 (4.672, 5.665, −6.764), row 173 (5.116, −8.368, −6.351), row 363 (… −6.437, 3.978, 9.762).
  - About 44 rows copy the b1 pattern: around −0.4 in column 17, −0.2 in columns 14 and 23, +0.2 in column 27. Examples are row 11 (… −0.455 … 0.180), row 150 (… −0.508 … 0.170) and row 386 (… −0.489 … 0.364). Inputs that are always 1 get the same gradient as the bias, so these are constant inputs. That is the 36 border-wall cells plus about 8 more fixed cells.
- **About 128 varying inputs (roughly 2 × 64).** This fits a small agent region plus one object that roams the board plus the previous-frame channel. An agent confined to one column gives about 8 inputs, a ball about 56–64, and the previous frame about 64.
- **Output layer: only three real choices.** The no-op, left and right columns of W3 move together:
  - h2 unit 6 gives [−1.048, 4.727, −0.121, −1.257, −1.371].
  - Unit 30 gives [0.574, −0.840, −2.691, 1.006, 1.142].
  - Unit 31 gives left 0.513 and right 0.185.

  Left and right are almost never set against each other. The big, distinct weights sit only in the up column (4.727, 1.302, 1.089, −1.603, −1.068) and the down column (1.526, 1.262, −2.691, −1.235, −1.160). So the policy is really choosing between up, down and "stay", which means horizontal moves have no effect.
- **Biases.** b3 = [−0.084, −0.819, −0.271, 0.916, 0.132]. Left gets the highest bias, but that only picks one of the equivalent "stay" actions.
- **Default behaviour.** b2[6] = −2.54, and the constant inputs push unit 6 further negative, so "up" (weight 4.727) is strongly suppressed by default. b2[30] = +1.71 suppresses "down" (weight −2.691). Up and down are both triggered by specific situations, and otherwise the agent stays put. That is tracking behaviour, not a constant push in one direction.

**Reasoning:**

- **Ruled out:**
  - Games where the agent moves sideways (Catch, Breakout, Space Invaders, a paddle at the bottom). Left and right are not used as distinct actions.
  - Free-roaming games (Snake, maze or collect-the-target games). These would make left and right matter, and would give about 192 varying inputs.
- **Freeway / road crossing.** The agent moves only vertically, and the 8 extra constants could be a goal row. But Freeway's optimal policy presses up almost all the time, while here up is suppressed by default and only fires on a cue. The varying-input count also fits poorly.
- **Pong.** A paddle confined to one column moves up or down to stay level with a ball that roams the board. This fits the vertical-only control, the symmetric "stay unless the ball is above or below" structure, and the roughly 8 + 56 + 64 varying inputs.
- **Side-scrolling dodger.** Dodging objects that move horizontally is the main alternative.
- **Weak point.** The roughly 8 extra constant inputs are not explained. They might be a fixed reward column or another fixed structure.

**Game:** A Pong-style paddle game (single-player Pong or squash against the wall). The agent is a vertical paddle in a fixed column.

**Description:** The agent moves its paddle up or down along one column to stay in line with a moving ball and send it back. Reward comes from hitting or returning the ball, and missing it ends the rally or episode.
