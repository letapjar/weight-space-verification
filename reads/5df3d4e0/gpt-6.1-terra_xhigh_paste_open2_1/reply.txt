That factorization alone would prove little. But the **placement of repeated weight patterns** makes it much more interesting.

- \(c\) is a grid column from 0 to 9;

The table shows only four components, but the resemblance extends across the vectors. I have not computed a formal correlation statistic; this is a pattern visible in the supplied numbers.

One possible explanation is that channel 3 indicates a fixed **wall or boundary**. If a feature is always 1 during training, its first-layer weight receives the same per-example gradient as the corresponding bias:

\[
\frac{\partial L}{\partial W_{1,ij}}=x_i\delta_j,
\qquad
\frac{\partial L}{\partial b_{1,j}}=\delta_j.
\]

When \(x_i=1\), those gradients match. Under suitable initialization and optimization conditions, the resulting weight row can come to resemble the bias, apart from its initial offset. Many permanently active border features could therefore produce rows resembling one another and `b1`.

That is a **plausible mechanism**, not proof. Deliberate construction, correlated inputs, or other training choices could also produce a related pattern.
