**Observations:**
- **The input weights retain a five-input block structure, but the cells appear spatially shuffled.** One offset has substantial learned weights at only eight inputs: **15, 65, 70, 140, 270, 300, 370, 485** (zero-based). For example, \(W1[15,0]=-5.593\) and \(W1[70,20]=5.126\). This looks like the **agent channel restricted to one eight-cell row**, rather than a character moving throughout the board.
- **Two other offsets look like current moving-object position and previous-frame position.** They have large weights across many more cells. For example, inputs **1 and 4** contain weights **−4.621** and **−4.635**, respectively; inputs **41 and 44** contain **6.260** and **−8.625**. Such paired, widespread sensitivity fits tracking a ball and its motion.
- **There is a separate, restricted set of reward-related inputs.** I count **24 clearly trained rows** at the target-like offset. Examples include \(W1[12,3]=-1.795\), \(W1[12,24]=-2.022\), and \(W1[47,24]=-2.620\). Other inputs at that offset remain around the small, initialization-like scale of roughly \(0.05\)–\(0.1\). Twenty-four target locations suggest **three rows of eight bricks**.
- **The obstacle-like offset contains 36 rows with a shared weight pattern**, matching the 36 always-present border cells. For example, \(W1[8,17]=-0.368\) and \(W1[23,17]=-0.323\), close to the corresponding bias \(b1[17]=-0.412\). This is consistent with constant border inputs receiving the same training updates as the bias.
- **The output layer mainly distinguishes left from right.** For example:
  - \(W3[16]=[-0.563,-0.551,-0.540,-1.002,\mathbf{2.903}]\)
  - \(W3[21]=[-0.870,-0.873,-0.615,\mathbf{2.306},-0.172]\)
  
  No-op, up, and down often receive similar contributions, while horizontal actions receive much stronger, differentiated contributions. The output biases, **[0.099, 0.276, −0.125, −0.058, 0.027]**, are comparatively small; the slightly positive up bias does **not** establish an upward-moving policy.

**Reasoning:**  
The combination is characteristic of a **horizontal paddle, a moving ball tracked using its previous position, and a separate array of stationary reward targets**.

A catching or dodging game could explain horizontal control and moving objects, but it fits the separate 24-location target array less well. In a simple falling-reward game, moving-object and reward channels would often mark the same objects, rather than showing these distinct patterns. Pong explains the paddle and ball, but not naturally three rows’ worth of reward targets.

Snake, maze navigation, and general collection games are strongly disfavored by the agent’s eight-location footprint and the output layer’s predominantly horizontal decisions. **Breakout explains all these observations together.** The weights do not uniquely determine every rule, but the structural match is strong.

**Game:** **Breakout — a grid-based brick-breaking game, likely with three rows of bricks and a bottom paddle.**

**Description:** The agent moves a paddle left and right to bounce a ball back into the playfield and keep it from escaping below. Reward comes from hitting and removing the bricks above.
