These readings depend on counting lines in a long matrix, so individual row assignments may be off by one. The broad pattern (ch1/ch4 in the upper block, ch0 and ch2 near the bottom) is the more reliable part.

**Reasoning**

The network treats the upper six rows as a hazard field of moving objects, with a reward target at the top. The agent is important mainly near the bottom. That fits a road-crossing game: the agent starts at the bottom, traffic moves in the middle lanes, and reward is at the top. It also fits the absence of interior obstacles, since a crossing game needs only the border as static walls.

Ruled out or weakened:
- Maze-based games (Pac-Man mazes, Sokoban): no interior ch3 weights.
- Frogger: its water hazards would need large interior ch3 weights, which are absent.
- Snake, and games where the agent roams the whole grid: agent weights are small in rows 1-6, which argues against free movement there.

The main weakness of a crossing game is that the agent's weights are small in the lanes, so the policy should not know its own lane well. A bottom-restricted catch or shooter game could also produce this pattern. I rank crossing highest but not decisively.

**Game**

Freeway-style road-crossing game (Atari Freeway / MinAtar-like), possibly with a safe row near the bottom. Lower-probability alternatives are a bottom-restricted catch or shooter game.

**Description**

The agent starts at the bottom and moves up through lanes of moving traffic, with the cars as the moving objects. It gets reward for reaching the reward target at the top, and a collision with a moving object ends the attempt or sends it back.
