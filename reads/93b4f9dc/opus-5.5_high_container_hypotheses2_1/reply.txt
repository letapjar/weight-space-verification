# Analysis of a 500 → 32 → 32 → 5 MLP

## TL;DR

This is very likely a small **policy network for a Breakout-style Atari game**.

- **Input:** a 10×10 grid with 5 one-hot channels per cell (100 × 5 = 500). The outer ring of cells is always "wall". The inner 8×8 is the playfield.
- **Channels:** paddle (bottom row only), trail or previous ball position (rows 1–7), bricks (top three rows only), wall (border only), and ball (rows 1–8).
- **Output:** 5 action scores. Three of them (0, 1, 2) behave almost identically, and two (3, 4) are opposite horizontal moves.

The rest of this note gives the evidence, in the order I found it.

---

## 1. Three kinds of rows in W1

Reading all 500 rows, each row falls cleanly into one of three groups:

| Type | Look | Count | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Init noise** | Values around ±0.06, max about 0.24, no structure | 312 | The input is never non-zero in training, so it never gets a gradient. These are the untouched initial weights. |
| **B. Copy of b1** | Nearly the same as b1 every time (e.g. col 4 ≈ −0.3 to −0.5, col 12 ≈ +0.3, col 30 ≈ −0.45, col 31 ≈ +0.4) | 36 | The input is **always 1**. Its gradient is then exactly the bias gradient, so the row equals b1 plus its own init noise. Example: row 3 minus b1 gives about ±0.07 per entry. |
| **C. Trained** | Magnitudes of 1–7.7 | 152 | Sparse inputs that are informative. |

A type-B input carries no information. Any input that is a constant 1 becomes a copy of the bias. That alone tells us the input is a structured encoding with a constant channel, not ordinary data features.

## 2. The period-5 structure gives 100 cells × 5 channels

The type-B rows sit at indices 3, 8, 13, 18, … (all ≡ 3 mod 5), so I wrote each index as `i = 5·cell + channel`. The 100 cells then form a 10×10 grid (`row = cell // 10`, `col = cell % 10`). Mapping every row this way gives:

| Grid row | Cols 0 and 9 | Cols 1–8: ch0 | ch1 | ch2 | ch3 | ch4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | wall | wall | wall | wall | wall | wall |
| 1–3 | ch3 = const | – | ✓ | ✓ | – | ✓ |
| 4–7 | ch3 = const | – | ✓ | – | – | ✓ |
| 8 | ch3 = const | ✓ | – | – | – | ✓ |
| 9 | wall | wall | wall | wall | wall | wall |

Here "wall" means the whole cell is border: only ch3 is on (the bias-copy rows), and the other four channels are dead.

The counts check out exactly:
- Trained rows: 24 × 3 + 32 × 2 + 8 × 2 = **152**.
- Border cells: **36**.
- Everything else (**312**) is dead.

A few row lookups as examples:
- Row 405 is cell 81, ch0 (bottom row, col 1).
- Row 57 is cell 11, ch2.
- Rows 98 and 103 are cells 19 and 20, ch3 (right and left wall).

Further observations:
- **Channel 3 = wall/off-board.** It is on for exactly the 36 border cells and never inside the field.
- **There is no "empty" channel.** None of the inner channels is dense or near-constant. A dense "empty" channel would look nearly bias-like, and none does. So an empty square is the all-zero vector.
- **The 8×8 cells inside the ring are the playfield.**

## 3. Hypotheses about the input domain

| Hypothesis | Verdict | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Tabular or generic features | **Ruled out** | The strict mod-5 period, the 2D layout, and the constant border cannot be generic features. |
| Natural images (pixels) | **Ruled out** | Inputs are one-hot with many never-active slots. Pixels are dense. |
| Chess / Othello / Go-like board game | **Ruled out** | Piece types would appear on all squares. Here every channel is tied to specific rows (ch0 only on row 8, ch2 only on rows 1–3). |
| Checkers | **Ruled out** | Checkers uses only dark squares. Here all 8 columns of every row are trained. |
| Gridworld navigation (MiniGrid-like, 5 actions) | **Partly** | The wall ring and 5 outputs fit. But a navigating agent can be anywhere, while ch0 is pinned to the bottom row. |
| Catch / Pong | **Unlikely** | Catch has no brick layer. Pong needs a second paddle at the top. |
| **Breakout-like** (paddle, ball, bricks, ball trail, walls) | **Best fit** | See below. |
| Space-Invaders-like (cannon, aliens, two bullet types) | **Runner-up** | See below. |

### Assigning the channels under the Breakout hypothesis

- **ch0 = paddle.** It appears only on the bottom row, in all 8 columns, and carries the largest weights in the network (7.72 at row 405).
- **ch2 = bricks.** They appear only in the top three rows, in all columns, with moderate and loosely structured weights. Bricks matter less for control.
- **ch4 = ball, ch1 = trail** (previous ball position).
  - ch1 and ch4 share the same spatial footprint, including the same weak spot. The top-left and top-right corners are barely trained in both (rows 56/59 max about 0.4; rows 91/94 max about 0.6–0.9). Those corners are rarely reached by a ball once the bricks are cleared.
  - The ball can enter the paddle row (a miss or terminal frame, which is rare, hence the moderate weights in rows 409–444). The trail never can, because a ball in row 8 doesn't continue to a next step. That decides the order: if ch1 were the ball, the trail could not appear in row 8 without the ball having been there first.
  - Trail and ball at the same cell have **opposite effects**. Example, cell 77: trail (row 386) gives h1-unit 1 = +4.29 and unit 6 = +3.99, while ball (row 389) gives −5.47 and −4.71. This is how the network reads ball direction, e.g. "just bounced off the paddle" versus "falling toward the paddle".
  - Both channels carry matching common-mode offsets on the same hidden units:
    - unit 11 is positive for almost every ball and trail cell;
    - units 1, 16, 17 and 19 are negative for almost all of them.
    - These cancel the large effective biases (see §4).

    That is what you would expect when exactly one ball and one trail are always present together.
- **Space Invaders, by contrast:** bullets come and go independently and would not share these tightly matched offsets. The aliens would also descend over time, but ch2 never leaves rows 1–3.

## 4. What the weights reveal about training

- **Initializer.** The dead rows look Gaussian with σ ≈ 0.06–0.07 and extremes around 0.24. That matches Glorot-normal (√(2/532) = 0.061) or He-normal (0.063). It rules out PyTorch's default Kaiming-uniform, whose bound is 1/√500 = 0.045.
- **Little or no weight decay.** The dead rows are still at init scale and the border rows still equal b1 plus noise. L2 decay would have shrunk both.
- **The real bias is about 37 × b1.** 36 border inputs plus the bias itself all act as a constant 1, so the effective layer-1 bias is about 37·b1. For example:
  - unit 30 ≈ −17
  - unit 4 ≈ −14
  - unit 31 ≈ +14.6
  - unit 12 ≈ +11.8

  The stored b1 looks small only because it has 36 duplicates.
- **Degenerate hidden units.**
  - Unit 3 has an effective bias of about −5.6 and tiny weights on every input: probably a dead ReLU.
  - Unit 20 has an effective bias of about +5 and also tiny input weights: it is always on and acts as a constant feeding layer 2.

## 5. The 5 outputs

From W3 and b3:
- **Outputs 0, 1 and 2 have nearly identical weights** on most layer-2 units. Examples:
  - unit 4: −0.78, −0.87, −0.82
  - unit 26: −0.81, −0.83, −0.83
  - unit 9: 0.57, 0.61, 0.67

  They differ only on a few units (2, 8, 15, 17, 20, 22, 25, 28).
- **Outputs 3 and 4 are opposites** of each other, with 0–2 sitting between them:
  - unit 4: +4.19 vs −0.97
  - unit 26: −1.15 vs +3.51
  - units 24, 29, 30 and 17 show the same anti-correlation.
- Output 4 has the largest bias (+0.39).

### Probing what separates 3 from 4

For several layer-1 units I computed a linear path score, `s_j = Σ_k W2[j,k]·(W3[k,4] − W3[k,3])`. This ignores the layer-2 ReLUs, so it is only rough. Positive means the unit pushes toward output 4.

| h1 unit | What it responds to most | s_j |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Paddle at col 1 (+7.7), col 2 (+2.75) | **+32** |
| 5 | Paddle ramp, high on the left | **+37** |
| 1 | Paddle left/middle (very negative at cols 6–8) | **+53** |
| 17 | Paddle *not* at the left (−3.4, −5.8 at cols 1–2) | **−55** |
| 18 | Paddle ramp, high on the right | **−28** |
| 21 | Paddle on the right **and** ball on the right (row 7) | **−16** |

The pattern is consistent: **left-side configurations push toward output 4, right-side configurations push toward output 3.** So 3 and 4 form a single horizontal axis that is driven mostly by **paddle position**. That points to paddle movement actions, not a prediction about the ball, since the ball's own motion would depend on the trail rather than on the paddle.

### Hypotheses for the outputs

- **Q-values (DQN): less likely.** Q-heads share a large common state-value direction across all actions. Here, the units that move output 3 do not move outputs 0–2 the same way, and outputs 3 and 4 are anti-correlated.
- **Five-way prediction of a game event: less likely**, for the reason above: paddle position dominates the 3-vs-4 axis.
- **Policy logits or a behavior-cloning classifier over 5 actions: most likely.**
  - Three actions have almost the same effect on the game, so they get almost the same logit. Examples would be NOOP/FIRE in Atari Breakout, or "stay/up/down" in a generic stay-up-down-left-right action set, where vertical moves do nothing to the paddle.
  - Outputs 3 and 4 are left and right.
  - The small differences among outputs 0–2 might matter in special cases, such as a FIRE/serve action.
  - The tentative direction is **output 4 = move right, output 3 = move left**, because a paddle at the far left pushes toward 4. This part is the least certain. The real decision has to use ball-minus-paddle position and ball direction (from the trail) together. Ball tuning in individual units is noisy (e.g. unit 9 favours both col 1 and col 5), and a linear analysis through two ReLU layers cannot settle it.

---

## Best explanation

This is a small ReLU MLP policy for a **custom 8×8 Breakout-like game wrapped in a 1-cell wall border** (a MinAtar-style one-hot grid with 5 channels: paddle, trail, brick, wall, ball). It was trained, probably with Adam and no weight decay from a Gaussian init (σ ≈ 0.06), to output scores over **5 actions**: three near-equivalent non-moving actions and two horizontal paddle moves (outputs 3 and 4).

Supporting evidence:
- 312 input slots are untouched by training because those objects can never appear there.
- The 36 always-on wall inputs are exact copies of the bias.
- Paddle, ball and trail carry the largest learned weights.
- Outputs 3 and 4 are opposite ends of a left–right axis driven by the paddle's position.

**Confidence:**
- High for the grid and channel structure.
- Fairly high for Breakout over Space Invaders.
- Moderate for the action interpretation of the outputs.
- Low for which of outputs 3 and 4 is left versus right.
